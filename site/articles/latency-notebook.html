A latency notebook, with the edges left in.

Three regional observations and the questions that a small performance report leaves open.

NPNoah ParkOct 05, 20264 min read

The observations

A fictional catalog service produced the following notebook entries during a short observation window. The values describe the entries, not a production benchmark or a provider comparison. Each row has a different sample count.

RegionRequestsMedianp95
Frankfurt12042 ms91 ms
Singapore8068 ms144 ms
Virginia10037 ms88 ms

There were 300 requests across the three rows. That arithmetic is available from the table. A global p95 is not. Regional percentiles cannot be averaged to reconstruct the percentile of the combined distribution.

What was not recorded

The notebook does not preserve individual samples, connection reuse, payload sizes, the proportion of cache hits, or a baseline deployment. It also omits the client’s distance from each region and whether warm-up requests were included.

These gaps prevent an attribution such as “the cache caused a thirty percent improvement.” No before-and-after series appears here. A claim about an improvement would require a comparable baseline and a description of what changed between the two observations.

A useful next measurement

Retain request-level timings with a region tag, status code, representation version, and a cache outcome when the platform exposes one. Compute combined percentiles from the samples. Keep failed requests in the record rather than silently dropping them.

{
  "region": "fra",
  "duration_ms": 42,
  "status": 200,
  "representation": "catalog-v42"
}

Until those observations exist, the table supports a narrow account: three regional medians, three regional p95 values, and a total of 300 requests. It leaves the global tail and any causal explanation unresolved.
